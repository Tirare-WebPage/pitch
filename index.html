TIRARE / FIELD NOTES / 2024—26A mechanical interface for the vehicle already at work.

The starting point

Keep the tricycle. Rethink the sprocket.

Tirare is developing an electric-assist retrofit built around MRII—the Mechanical Rider Intent Interface.

Electric vehicles already exist. So do conversion kits. The hard question is how to make assistance work with the drivetrain a worker already owns.

03 / START FROM THE MACHINE

What the first design taught us

Before adding power, follow the torque.

The reference tricycle already uses a ratchet/freewheel: it passes rider power to the wheel but allows the wheel to keep turning without forcing the pedals to follow. The design challenge is to integrate rider-effort sensing and motor assistance around that existing one-way behaviour.

01Pedals + crankRider applies force.
02ChainCarries rider input.
03SprocketTurns the drive.
04Ratchet / freewheelAllows one-way overrun.
05Axle + wheelsVehicle moves.

The freewheel is already there. What else must the system do?

The tricycle already has a one-way mechanism. The harder design task is adding rider-effort sensing and motor assistance while keeping the one-way drive behaviour working as intended.

MRII is designed to work with this existing idea—not to claim the freewheel itself as new.

Newton’s third law / reaction torque

The axle turns. The motor mount takes the reaction.

When the motor applies torque to the axle, it applies an equal and opposite reaction torque to its housing. The mount has to carry that load into the frame.

These tricycles have spent years in rain, on rough roads and under heavy loads. Some have wooden frames. We cannot assume an old frame is a safe motor mount just because the wheel turns.

The motor, mounting and frame need a deliberate force path—and testing on the real vehicle.

05 / MRII IN MOTION

Mechanical Rider Intent Interface

One interface. Three jobs.

MRII is designed to replace the driven sprocket with a mechanical interface that transmits rider torque, reads rider effort through a tiny controlled movement, and separates the rider side from powered rotation.

ONE INTERFACE / THREE STATES

STATE 01 / ENGAGE

When the rider drives, the one-way element engages.

Human torque travels from the pedals through MRII toward the axle. The small flexure movement is measured as the rider-effort signal.

Conceptual force-path drawing, not a manufacturing drawing. The integrated vehicle must validate engagement, overrun, manual operation and failure behaviour.

1× rider torque →
1× assist requestHuman torque + motor assistance at the axle

Human contribution remains in the mechanical path. The motor adds support downstream. The system is designed so motor-side rotation doesn't simply become pedal-side motion.

01

Engage

The rider-side input drives the one-way element in its locking direction, transmitting torque to the axle.

02

Sense

Flexures deform slightly. The magnetic target shifts relative to the encoder; the calibrated angle estimates rider input.

03

Assist

The intended control principle makes motor assistance respond to rider effort, rather than simply commanding a speed.

06 / WHAT CHANGES

What MRII changes

The clever part is what it lets us remove.

MRII is not a bigger motor or another dashboard. It changes the interface between the rider and powered axle—so rider effort can become the assistance reference mechanically.

01 / TRANSMITCarry the rider's torque.
02 / SENSERead rider effort from controlled flexure.
03 / SEPARATEAllow powered rotation to overrun the rider side.
01 / SIZING

Size for assistance

The motor is intended to supplement the rider—not replace all human propulsion. Final sizing depends on load, grade and duty cycle.

02 / ENERGY

Less energy to store

Lower electrical energy demand could reduce battery capacity, vehicle mass and the capital tied up in the battery.

03 / HARDWARE

No separate rider-assist controller

MRII is designed to remove the separate rider-torque-sensor module and rider-assist controller layer: the mechanism itself produces the rider-intent signal. The motor still requires its power stage, current limiting and electrical safety protection.

04 / FIT

Keep the vehicle intact

MRII is designed to replace the driven sprocket—not the chassis, cargo bed, wheels or entire vehicle. The full retrofit still requires a motor package and mounting.

The 1:1 principle

1:1 torque assistance is the intended starting point.

At the intended 1:1 setting, the calibrated rider-torque signal requests matching motor-assist torque, within operating limits. That is a torque-assist ratio—not a claim that we have already demonstrated the complete vehicle at that setting.

The controller distinction

Simplify the assist layer.

MRII is designed to remove the separate conventional rider-torque sensor and dedicated assist-level control layer: rider effort is encoded mechanically at the interface. This does not remove the motor power controller, current limiting, emergency disconnect or other electrical safety requirements.

07 / ACCESS & PAYMENTS

Affordability is a product decision and a payment decision

The operator should not have to finance the whole transition at once.

The operator already owns the vehicle. Tirare's proposed model keeps that asset in service and separates access to assistance from battery ownership.

01 / KEEP

Keep the tricycle

The worker keeps the existing vehicle, familiar work and customer relationships.

02 / INSTALL

Local workshop

A trained workshop installs and maintains the retrofit with planned downtime.

03 / ACCESS

Battery service

A managed battery pool and swap process means the operator need not purchase the battery outright.

04 / PAY

As work comes in

Recurring daily or weekly charges are designed around cash flow and actual willingness to pay.

The unit isn't just a kit. It's access.

This approach can reduce the upfront barrier while preserving local servicing. It also creates recurring revenue to support battery use, maintenance and customer support.

The economics still have to work.

The pilot must measure battery utilisation, swap frequency, service costs, downtime, customer retention and willingness to pay. A recurring-payment design is a hypothesis until operators use it and the numbers balance.

09 / WHAT MUST BE PROVEN

The work ahead

Now the work leaves the screen.

The next chapter is not another claim. It's a sequence of tests that can show what works, what fails and what needs to change.

Gate 01 / Bench

Prove the mechanism.

Test MRII independently with recorded inputs, known torque and repeatable conditions.

  • Torque signal and calibration
  • Engagement and overrun behaviour
  • Drag, wear, heat and failure modes
Gate 02 / Integration

Prove the assembly.

Fit the motor, reduction stage and MRII to a reference vehicle without compromising the underlying tricycle.

  • Mount and axle loads
  • Chain alignment and braking
  • Manual rideability with assist off
Gate 03 / Field

Measure the work.

Only after engineering review and the appropriate safety/regulatory pathway, trial with consenting operators and trained support.

  • Uptime and energy use
  • Payload, trips and operating cost
  • Fatigue and willingness to pay

The goal is not to skip the hard part. The goal is to make the next test focused enough to teach us something decisive.

Tirare / The premise

The vehicle is already earning. Change the interface, not the livelihood.

We are developing a way to bring proportional electric assistance to vehicles that already work for a living. The next step is to test the integrated system—and report what it proves.

Our proposed battery-swapping service would let the worker pay smaller daily or weekly amounts instead of buying the battery upfront. That payment model still needs testing.

3:50–4:25 / Two years of work

Support—and responsibility.

We've spent two years building and changing our designs, and have built and demonstrated the MRII physical subsystem. IIIT Delhi awarded us a ₹1 lakh prototype grant. On 2 October, we filed a patent application. Our counsel reduced his fees by ₹30,000 and offered to approach TVS about testing. Professor Andy Ruina has also provided technical guidance. We're grateful for the support—but the complete vehicle still has to prove itself.

4:25–5:00 / The next proof

Change the part, not the livelihood.

Next we need to make sure the mechanism reads rider effort correctly, works under load, and allows manual riding when assistance is off. Then we need to test it with workers and measure reliability, energy use, physical effort and running cost. We estimate around 13 lakh potential users; we haven't reached them yet. We must earn that opportunity by proving the technology. A worker shouldn't have to replace the vehicle that feeds their family just to make the work easier. The vehicle is already earning. Let's make it work better for the person who depends on it.
